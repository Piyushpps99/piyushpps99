UNIT I — Management: Meaning, Need, Scope & Thinkers

1.1 Meaning and Definition of Management

Management means getting work done through other people in a proper and planned way. It is the art and science of planning, organizing, directing and controlling the efforts of people so that the goals of an organization are achieved.

Simple Meaning
Management is about deciding what to do, how to do it, who will do it, and checking that it is done correctly.

Harold Koontz: “Management is the art of getting things done through and with people in formally organized groups.”

Henry Fayol: “To manage is to forecast and plan, to organize, to command, to coordinate and to control.”

Peter Drucker: “Management is a multi-purpose organ that manages a business, manages managers and manages workers and work.”

Mary Parker Follett: “Management is the art of getting things done through people.”

Key Idea
A manager does not do all the work himself. He plans the work, gives work to the right people, guides them, and checks the results.

1.2 Need and Importance of Management

  • Achievement of Goals — Helps reach organizational goals in a planned way.
  • Optimum Use of Resources — Money, men, machines and materials are used carefully.
  • Minimizes Cost — Good planning and control reduce waste.
  • Creates Healthy Relations — Builds good relations among workers and managers.
  • Adapts to Change — Helps the organization survive in a changing world.
  • Development of Society — Creates jobs and useful products.
  • Provides Leadership — Guides and motivates employees.
  • Innovation — Encourages new ideas and better ways of working.

1.3 Scope of Management

Management is needed in almost every field.

Functional Areas (inside a business):

  • Production Management
  • Marketing Management
  • Financial Management
  • Human Resource Management
  • Purchase & Materials Management

Also used in schools, hospitals, government offices, banks, army, NGOs, sports teams, and even families.

1.4 The Process of Management (Functions)

Management is a continuous process. The main functions (often called POSDCORB or the five functions) are:

  1. Planning — Deciding in advance what to do, how, when and by whom.
  2. Organizing — Arranging people, money and materials; creating departments and roles.
  3. Staffing — Finding the right people, training them and keeping them happy.
  4. Directing (Leading) — Guiding, motivating and supervising employees.
  5. Controlling — Checking actual work against the plan and taking corrective action.
Remember
These functions are continuous and interconnected. A manager plans → organizes → directs → controls → and then plans again based on results.

1.5 Development of Management Thought

A. F.W. Taylor — Scientific Management (1856–1915)

Called the Father of Scientific Management. He believed work should be studied scientifically to find the best way of doing it.

Main Principles:

1. Science, not rule of thumb — Study the work scientifically; do not rely on old habits.
2. Harmony, not discord — Managers and workers should work as a team.
3. Cooperation, not individualism — Both sides should help each other.
4. Development of each person to his greatest efficiency — Select and train workers carefully.

Techniques: Time Study, Motion Study, Standardization, Differential Piece Rate System, Functional Foremanship.

Simple Example
Instead of asking workers to shovel coal any way they like, Taylor studied the best size of shovel and the best body movements so a worker could move more coal with less tiredness.

B. Henry Fayol — Administrative Management (1841–1925)

Called the Father of Modern Management. Focused on top managers and the whole organization. He identified 5 functions: Planning, Organizing, Commanding, Coordinating, Controlling.

14 Principles of Management:

1. Division of Work — Specialize; each person becomes expert → higher efficiency.
2. Authority and Responsibility — Both should go together.
3. Discipline — Employees must obey rules and agreements.
4. Unity of Command — One employee should receive orders from only one boss.
5. Unity of Direction — Activities with the same objective should have one plan and one head.
6. Subordination of Individual Interest — Organization’s interest comes first.
7. Remuneration — Pay should be fair to both employee and company.
8. Centralization — Balance the concentration of authority.
9. Scalar Chain — Clear chain of authority from top to bottom.
10. Order — A place for everything and everything in its place.
11. Equity — Managers should be kind and fair; no favoritism.
12. Stability of Tenure — Job security increases efficiency.
13. Initiative — Encourage employees to think of new ideas.
14. Esprit de Corps — Team spirit; “Union is strength.”

C. Hawthorne Studies — Human Relations Approach (1924–1932)

Conducted at the Western Electric Hawthorne plant (Chicago) under Elton Mayo. These studies changed how managers think about workers.

Main Findings:

  • Workers are not machines; they have social and emotional needs.
  • When workers feel management is paying attention (the “Hawthorne Effect”), performance improves.
  • Informal groups strongly influence how much a worker produces.
  • Good human relations, recognition and belonging are vital for high productivity.
Importance
After Hawthorne, management started giving importance to human feelings, group behavior, communication and leadership — not only to money and working conditions.

UNIT II — Planning & Organizing

2.1 Planning — Need, Scope and Importance

Meaning: Planning means thinking before doing. It is deciding in advance what is to be done, how, when and by whom.

Need and Importance:

  • Provides Direction
  • Reduces Risk and Uncertainty
  • Minimizes Waste
  • Encourages Innovation
  • Helps in Coordination
  • Facilitates Control
  • Improves Decision Making
  • Motivates Employees

Scope: Strategic plans (top), tactical plans (middle), operational plans (lower). Covers production, marketing, finance, HR, etc.

2.2 Steps in Planning

  1. Setting Objectives — Specific, measurable, time-bound goals.
  2. Developing Planning Premises — Assumptions about the future.
  3. Identifying Alternatives — List possible courses of action.
  4. Evaluating Alternatives — Compare advantages, cost, risk.
  5. Selecting the Best Alternative — Choose the plan.
  6. Formulating Supporting Plans — Departmental plans.
  7. Implementing the Plan
  8. Follow-up / Review — Check and adjust.

2.3 Decision Making Model

Decision making is choosing the best course of action from available alternatives — the heart of management.

  1. Identify the Problem
  2. Gather Information
  3. Generate Alternatives
  4. Evaluate Alternatives
  5. Select the Best Alternative
  6. Implement the Decision
  7. Evaluate the Results

Types: Programmed (routine) vs Non-programmed (unique); Strategic, Tactical, Operational.

2.4 Organizing — Need and Importance

Organizing means arranging resources and creating a structure of roles and relationships so plans can be carried out effectively.

  • Helps implement plans
  • Defines authority and responsibility clearly
  • Avoids duplication of work
  • Creates coordination
  • Facilitates specialization
  • Helps growth
  • Improves communication

2.5 Organizational Design and Structure

  • Line Structure — Simple chain of command; one boss. Suitable for small firms.
  • Line and Staff — Line managers decide; staff specialists advise.
  • Functional Structure — Divided by functions (Production, Marketing, Finance, HR).
  • Divisional Structure — By products, regions or customers.
  • Matrix Structure — Dual reporting (functional + project boss).

2.6 Centralization and Decentralization

PointCentralizationDecentralization
Decision makingAt the top onlyShared with lower levels
SpeedCan be slowUsually faster
Suitable forSmall firms, crisisLarge, diverse firms
MotivationLower for juniorsHigher for juniors
ControlEasier for topNeeds good systems
Note
Complete centralization or complete decentralization is rare. Most organizations seek a balance.

2.7 Delegation of Authority

Delegation means a manager gives some authority and responsibility to a subordinate. The manager remains accountable for the final result.

Elements: Authority · Responsibility · Accountability

Why important? Reduces manager’s burden, develops subordinates, speeds decisions, improves motivation, helps succession planning.

Barriers: Fear of losing control, lack of trust, fear of mistakes, unclear instructions.

UNIT III — Directing & Controlling

3.1 Motivation — Meaning, Need and Importance

Motivation is the inner force that makes a person work hard towards a goal. It answers: “Why should I work hard?”

  • High motivation → high performance and productivity
  • Reduces absenteeism and turnover
  • Creates positive work environment
  • Helps accept change more easily
  • Better customer service and quality
  • Reduces need for close supervision

3.2 Theories of Motivation

A. Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs

  1. Physiological Needs — food, water, shelter, sleep
  2. Safety Needs — job security, protection, health insurance
  3. Social Needs — belonging, friendship, acceptance
  4. Esteem Needs — respect, recognition, status, achievement
  5. Self-Actualization — becoming the best one can be; growth, creativity
Manager’s Lesson
Find which need level the employee is on and provide the matching motivator (salary, security, team feeling, recognition, challenging work).

B. Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory

  • Hygiene Factors (salary, conditions, policy, security, supervision) — prevent dissatisfaction but do not strongly motivate.
  • Motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, growth) — truly motivate and create satisfaction.
Simple Takeaway
Fix hygiene factors to avoid unhappiness. Give interesting work, recognition and growth opportunities to create real motivation.

C. McGregor’s Theory X and Theory Y

  • Theory X — Workers dislike work, avoid responsibility, need force and control (leads to autocratic style).
  • Theory Y — Workers can enjoy work, seek responsibility, are creative and self-controlled (leads to participative style).

McGregor preferred Theory Y. Modern management largely follows Theory Y ideas.

3.3 Leadership — Meaning, Styles & Qualities

Leadership is the ability to influence and guide people so they willingly work towards goals. A manager has formal authority; a leader has influence even without it. Best managers are also good leaders.

Leadership Styles:

  • Autocratic — Leader decides alone; useful in emergencies; can demotivate long-term.
  • Democratic / Participative — Discusses with team; builds commitment; takes more time.
  • Laissez-faire — Full freedom to team; works only with highly skilled, motivated people.
  • Transformational — Inspires with vision; creates enthusiasm for change.
  • Transactional — Focuses on rewards and punishments based on performance.

Qualities of an Effective Leader: Vision, honesty, integrity, confidence, communication skills, empathy, emotional intelligence, decisiveness, courage, ability to inspire, humility, competence.

3.4 Principles of Directing

  • Maximum Individual Contribution
  • Harmony of Objectives
  • Unity of Command
  • Appropriate Direction Technique
  • Managerial Communication
  • Use of Informal Organization
  • Leadership
  • Follow-through

3.5 Controlling — Process & Techniques

Controlling means measuring actual performance, comparing it with standards, and taking corrective action.

Basic Control Process:

  1. Setting Standards
  2. Measuring Actual Performance
  3. Comparing Actual with Standards
  4. Analyzing Causes of Deviation
  5. Taking Corrective Action

Important Techniques: Budgetary Control, Break-even Analysis, Statistical Control, Quality Control (TQM, Six Sigma), Inventory Control (EOQ, ABC, JIT), PERT/CPM, MIS, Personal Observation, Ratio Analysis.

UNIT IV — Introduction to Communication

4.1 What is Communication & Process

Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, feelings and understanding. It succeeds only when the receiver understands the message as the sender intended.

Elements of the Process:

  1. Sender (Encoder)
  2. Message
  3. Encoding
  4. Channel / Medium
  5. Receiver (Decoder)
  6. Decoding
  7. Feedback
  8. Noise (any disturbance)

4.2 Levels and Barriers to Communication

Levels: Intrapersonal · Interpersonal · Group · Organizational / Mass

Common Barriers:

  • Language / Semantic barriers
  • Physical barriers (noise, distance, equipment)
  • Psychological barriers (emotions, prejudice, selective listening)
  • Organizational barriers (too many levels, information overload)
  • Cultural barriers
  • Personal barriers (attitude, lack of trust, ego)
How to Overcome
Use simple language, listen actively, take feedback, choose the right medium, be aware of cultural differences, reduce noise, and build trust.

4.3 Non-verbal Communication

  • Body Language (posture, gestures, facial expressions, eye contact)
  • Appearance (dress, grooming)
  • Proximity / Space
  • Touch (culturally appropriate)
  • Paralinguistics (tone, pitch, volume, speed, pauses)
  • Time (punctuality)

If words and body language contradict, people usually believe the body language.

4.4 Flow of Communication in Organizations

  • Downward — From higher to lower levels (orders, policies, goals).
  • Upward — From lower to higher levels (reports, suggestions, grievances).
  • Lateral / Horizontal — Between people or departments at the same level (coordination).
Key Point
A healthy organization needs free flow in all three directions. Blocking any one creates problems.

4.5 Technology Enabled Communication

Email, video conferencing, instant messaging, intranet, cloud tools, etc.

Impact: Speed, cost reduction, remote work, better records — but also information overload and reduced face-to-face interaction.

Choose the medium based on urgency, complexity, need for feedback, confidentiality, number of receivers, and need for a permanent record. Sensitive matters are still better handled face-to-face or by phone.

UNIT V — Business Writing & Soft Skills

5.1 Business Letters

A formal written message for official purposes that creates a permanent record.

  • Sales Letters — Follow AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action); highlight benefits.
  • Credit Letters — Granting, refusing or collecting credit; collection letters (mild → medium → strong).
  • Claim & Adjustment Letters — Customer states problem and requests remedy; company replies politely (grant, partial, or refuse with reasons).
  • Job Application / Cover Letter & Resume — Tailored to the job; resume sections: Contact, Objective, Education, Experience, Skills, Projects, Achievements (1–2 pages).

5.2 Reports — Types, Structure & Writing

Types: Informational, Analytical, Routine/Periodic, Special, Formal, Informal.

Structure of a Formal Report:

  1. Title Page / Cover
  2. Letter of Transmittal
  3. Table of Contents
  4. List of Figures / Tables
  5. Abstract / Executive Summary
  6. Introduction
  7. Body (methodology, findings, discussion)
  8. Conclusions and Recommendations
  9. References / Bibliography
  10. Appendices

Style: Clear, concise, objective, factual, impersonal, proper headings, short paragraphs, visual aids where helpful.

5.3 Technical Proposals

A formal offer to do a job, solve a problem or supply something. Types: Internal/External, Solicited/Unsolicited.

Main Parts: Title, Executive Summary, Introduction, Objectives, Proposed Plan / Methodology, Timeline, Budget, Benefits, Qualifications, Conclusion & Call to Action, Appendices.

5.4 Body Language & Dimensions of Speech

Body Language: Stand/sit straight, eye contact, open gestures, avoid fidgeting, match facial expression with words.

Dimensions of Speech: Syllable, Accent (word stress), Pitch, Rhythm, Intonation.

Paralinguistic Features: Volume, speed, pauses, tone, clarity, silence — these strongly affect how the message is received.

5.5 Presentation, GD, Interview & Events

Presentation Strategies: Know audience & purpose; clear structure (Opening → Body → Closing); simple language + visual aids; practice; eye contact; strong opening and closing.

Group Discussion (GD): Listen carefully, speak relevantly, do not dominate or stay silent, support with logic, be polite, help the group reach a conclusion.

Interview Skills: Research company & role; prepare common answers; use STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result); dress formally; be honest and confident; ask sensible questions.

Workshop / Seminar / Conference:

  • Workshop — Hands-on practical learning.
  • Seminar — Focused discussion/presentation on a specific topic.
  • Conference — Larger formal gathering to present research, share ideas and network.
